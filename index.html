A Natural Language Analysis of American Environmental Legislation, 1973-2025

American environmentalism has never been a single, stable project. Since the early 1970s, popular U.S. environmental politics has travelled between cleaning up spectacular pollution events and wilderness protection to technocratic regulation, to market-based climate policy, ideas of language of justice, resilience, and transition. Across that half-century, activists, agencies, and legislators have repeatedly redefined what “the environment” is about: the animals, the air, the water, the corporations, or the people.

Background

A central challenge is that the legal record of this movement is vast and uneven. The period from 1973–2025 contains landmark statutes, minor amendments, and bills where environmental provisions are buried alongside unrelated policies. Traditional qualitative approaches tend to gravitate toward well-known laws such as the Clean Air Act or the Endangered Species Act. As a result, our historical narratives about American environmentalism often rest on selectively sampled texts, rather than a systematic view of how the language of environmental governance has evolved across the entire federal statutory landscape.

Natural Language Processing (NLP) is a branch of artificial intelligence and computational linguistics that enables computers to represent, analyze, and derive meaning from human language in text or speech form. By treating environmental laws as a large text corpus, NLP methods can quantify how certain themes, for example, pollution control, risk management, climate mitigation, adaptation, or environmental justice, change in statutory language over time. Rather than replacing close reading, NLP provides a complementary, corpus-level view: it surfaces patterns and changes in the language of American environmentalism that can then be interpreted in light of social movements, scientific developments, and political change.

Natural Language Processing

Natural Language Processing (NLP) is a field at the intersection of computer science, linguistics, and machine learning that focuses on building computational methods to represent and analyze human language as data. In practice, NLP techniques transform unstructured text (in this project, laws) into numerical representations that algorithms can cluster and classify at scale. For policy research, it enables us to build on close reading of a handful of documents to systematic analysis of thousands of pages of legal text.

NLP is increasingly applied to environmental policy as a means of handling the volume and complexity of relevant text. One prominent line of work uses NLP pipelines to speed up and standardize the identification and classification of policies. Given a large collection of legal documents, these pipelines can scrape text, then either classify documents into predefined categories (e.g., “climate mitigation policy,” “adaptation plan,” “land restoration incentive”) or discover latent topics using unsupervised methods. This has been used, for example, to separate climate‑relevant policies that may be hidden in broader legislative corpora, to catalogue the types of incentives used in land‑restoration, Firebanks-Quevedo et al., 2022 and to map how often different instruments such as tax credits, standards, subsidies, informational tools, are mentioned in policy texts. Aubakirov et al., 2025 Thus, these algorithms quickly surface patterns and candidate documents that would otherwise be missed or take weeks of manual screening.

In this project, we employ two NLP techniques for legislative exploration: topic modelling and word embedding. These enable us to examine trends in language between years, as well as how meaning develops across time.

Methods

Data Processing

Natural language processing requires a text corpus on which to train the linguistic data. To do this, I used congress.gov's web API to access full-text legislation. These data must be comprehensive; anything not included in the dataset will not be accounted for in the analysis. Therefore, I used the following metrics to pull all relevant legislation that pertained to environmentalism:

1. Type: legislation
2. Congress: all
3. Status of legislation: Became Law
4. Policy area: Environmental Policy



This search yielded 214 bills passed between 1973 to 2025. Notably, this congressional record did not include bills before 1973, thus resulting in a computational blind spot in early 1970s legislation. The full list of federal legislation can be accessed here.

These texts then needed to be processed for language-learning. Until 1983, these documents were stored as pdf forms in two columns: one that is an annotation and one that is body text. This was a key issue in creating the text corpus, as differentiating between the main text and annotations is an open question in the world of text mining. However, by matching these columns to regex (“regular expressions”) that followed the rough column format, I was able to clean these annotations. After 1983, the text was stored as htm files, which did not face the same problem of column-cleaning.

To make the text usable in an NLP context, we further had to process individual words. This included:

1. Removing punctuation
2. Separating conjunctions into their constituent words
3. Removing “stop words,” or high-frequency, low-meaning words (ex. And, in, the, an, at, a)
4. Stemming words, or creating a consistent grammatical form (ex. Bike, biking, biked all become bik)
5. Lemmatizing, a more “refined” stemming, that creates a common grammatical form that considers context (e.x., "saw" as a noun stays "saw", but as a verb becomes "see")

These preprocessing steps make text more uniform such that NLP models can recognize patterns in meaning instead of being distracted by surface-level differences like punctuation. They also reduce sparsity in the data by collapsing many grammatical variants and filler words into a smaller, more informative vocabulary, which improves statistical reliability and model performance. Finally, operations like stemming and lemmatization group together words that express the same underlying concept, so that classifiers track ideas (bike/biking/biked) rather than treating every inflected form as unrelated.


TF-IDF for topic modelling

The core of NLP is vectorizing language; in other words, creating numerical representations of words, sentences, or documents.

Term frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) Jones, 1988 is a classical method for making documents into numerical vectors by weighing words according to how often they appear in a document (term frequency) and how rare it is across all documents in a corpus (inverse document frequency). Thus, if a word is frequent in a given text but rare in others, then it will receive a high TF-IDF score. This is repeated for all words in the corpus vocabulary, creating a numerical vector representation of a document by capturing its distinctive language.

These vectors can then be used to conduct topic modelling, where a model attempts to discover latent “topics” across documents by looking at word co-occurrence. Algorithms such as Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) Master, 2018 treat each document as a mixture of a small number of topics and each topic as a distribution over words, allowing summarization of large corpora in terms of interpretable themes (for example, “pollution control,” “climate mitigation,” “environmental justice”) and quantify how strongly each document expresses each theme.


Word2Vec

Word2vec Mikolov et al., 2013 (word to vector) is a family of neural network-based models that learn vector representations, or “embeddings,” for words based on interpreted meaning. This requires training on a large text corpora, during which the model “learns” similar words, then predicting a word from its immediately surrounding context.



This representation is distributional: words that appear in similar contexts end up with similar vectors in a high‑dimensional space, so geometric closeness encodes semantic and syntactic similarity. Because these embeddings capture regularities in language, simple vector arithmetic can reflect analogies (for example, the classic observation that the vector for “king” minus “man” plus “woman” lands near “queen”), which illustrates how relational meaning is encoded in the learned space.

After training a word2vec model, we can compare embeddings for words across documents, or, in this case, years. Since similarity correlates to geometry, we can compute distances (e.g., cosine similarity) between two vectors to see how closely-related they are in the semantic space. In this project, I use these comparisons to quantify how concepts cluster—say, whether “market,” “climate,” and “innovation” are near each other— and track whether words like “communities,” “equity,” or “resilience” move toward different associations over time.


Interpretation

Topic modelling outputs key ideas for a time period; word2vec provides the semantic associations defining said period.

Thus, to conduct interpretations of these time periods, I first examined 1) dominant themes of each time period using topic modelling, then 2) the semantic space from key topics. This provided 2 case studies for further investigation and exploration.

Results


Case study 1: Air Particulates, Acid Rain, and the Ozone Layer


Topic modelling identified “ozone” as a significant keyword in the 1980s, prompting a closer examination of its semantic role across time. Interestingly, ozone appears as a legislative keyword—that is, one of the 5,000 most prevalent terms— during just three periods: the 1970s, the late 1980s, and the early 1990s. This pattern suggests that ozone emerged as a policy problem relatively quickly and was then absorbed into the broader regulatory framework. However, its semantic associations reveal the gradual maturation of ecological regulation and environmental law.

In the early 1970s, ozone’s semantic associations are not linked to pollution or environmental harm. Instead, it appears alongside legal and administrative terminology such as “section” and emissions-source categories like “light-duty” vehicles. This positioning suggests that ozone initially occupied a relatively marginal place within environmental regulation, as seen in the Clean Air Act. Rather than functioning as an independent pollutant category, it appeared as part of broader discussions about emissions controls and regulatory classification. The embedding space therefore recovers an early legal history in which ozone was treated indirectly, before scientific and public concern surrounding ozone depletion and atmospheric chemistry had fully entered legislative language.

Sulfur dioxide, by contrast, occupies a far more established semantic role in the 1970s corpus. Its closest associations (“particulate,” “oxide,” “ambient,” and “pollutant”) closely mirror the vocabulary of early Clean Air Act implementation. This reflects the Act’s designation of sulfur dioxide and particulate matter Ross, Chmiel, and Ferkol, 2012 as criteria pollutants regulated under the National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS). The presence of terms such as “promulgate” and “judgement” further captures the statutory logic of the period, in which the EPA was tasked with legislating standards necessary to protect public health and welfare. Unlike ozone, whose early associations remain embedded in fragmented legal terminology, sulfur dioxide was already tightly integrated into the conceptual architecture of environmental regulation. Its semantic field reflects how SO₂ became one of the paradigmatic pollutants through which the NAAQS system itself was developed.

By the 1980s, however, ozone’s semantic neighborhood begins to resemble that of sulfur dioxide, reflecting a broader historical shift in environmental governance. During this period, international concern surrounding the ozone layer intensified through developments such as the 1979 revision of the ozone NAAQS, Sacramento Metropolitan Air Quality Management District, 2017 and the diplomatic lead-up to the 1987 Montreal Protocol. Correspondingly, ozone becomes more closely associated with terms such as “carbon,” “dioxide,” “oxides,” and “monoxide.” These associations suggest that ozone had, by this stage, become consolidated as a recognized source of environmental harm within a matured vocabulary of air-quality management.

The 1980s also mark an important transition in the semantic role of sulfur dioxide. Whereas earlier associations centered on automobile emissions and breathable air, SO₂ increasingly appears within the language of acid rain and industrial production EPA . Terms such as “acid,” “ammonia,” “production,” “manufacture,” and “facilities” reflect growing concern over acid deposition and emissions from power plants and heavy industry. Here, the model captures a second phase of sulfur regulation, in which Congress and the EPA link sulfur emissions to broader industrial and chemical systems, anticipating the acid-rain provisions LaCount et al., 2022 formalized in the 1990 Clean Air Act amendments.

By the 1980s, both ozone and sulfur dioxide share regulatory terminology such as “standards,” “primary,” and “secondary.” This convergence suggests that each pollutant had become integrated into a stable legal and scientific framework in which acceptable thresholds, ambient concentrations, and source categories were increasingly standardized.

Together, these semantic trajectories reveal a broader history of American air-quality regulation; sulfur dioxide entered environmental law early as a paradigmatic pollutant around which regulatory structures were initially built, while ozone emerged later as an object of concern with its own standards, classifications, and co-pollutants. The convergence of their semantic neighborhoods in the 1980s marks the moment at which late twentieth-century environmentalism stabilized a common regulatory language rooted in the crises of smog, acid rain, and ozone depletion.

Case study 2: Pollution


Like ozone, pollution was a prevalent theme in the topic modelling results. Unlike ozone and sulfur dioxide, however, it appeared across every year in the dataset, providing a key example for temporal investigation. The semantic neighborhood around “pollution” appears to move from a vocabulary of toxicity and regulation toward one of management, prevention, and technical administration.

In the 1970s and early 1980s, the strongest semantic associations with “pollution” are terms such as “toxic” and “control.” This reflects the historical context of the environmental movement that emerged after events such as the publication of Silent Spring and growing public anxiety over industrial contamination. Early federal statutes such as the before-mentioned Clean Air Act and the Clean Water Act framed pollution as an immediate and dangerous public threat requiring aggressive federal intervention. In their book Understanding Command and Control Hays and Rome, 2006 , historians Samuel Hays and Adam Rome describe this era as one of “command-and-control” environmentalism, in which pollution was conceived primarily as a visible industrial hazard that could be reduced through centralized regulation and enforcement. The prominence of “toxic” in the results thus aligns with the emergence of hazardous waste politics in the late 1970s and 1980s, culminating in Superfund legislation after Love Canal and other contamination crises.

By the 1990s and 2000s, however, the semantic landscape shifts. “Control” remains important, but “pollution” becomes associated less with catastrophe and more with management. During this period, federal policy increasingly emphasized risk assessment, cost-benefit analysis, Gundlach and Livermore, 2022 and emissions trading Chan et al., 2012 rather than purely punitive regulation. The rise of neoliberal governance under both Democratic and Republican administrations reframed environmental protection as a matter of efficient administration and technological optimization. Scholars have argued that environmental governance in this period shifted toward beaurocratic networks Dillon et al., 2018 between regulators, engineers, scientists, and corporations. Movement toward terms like “engineer,” “control,” and “prevent” reflects this transformation from an activist vocabulary to a mechanical one.

The appearance of words such as “tribe” and “Indian” into the 2020s suggests that environmental legislation increasingly incorporated Indigenous sovereignty and environmental justice concerns into discourse surrounding pollution. Fernández‐Llamazares et al., 2020 Since the late twentieth century, Native American tribes have become central actors Vickery and Hunter, 2017 in debates over pipelines, water rights, land stewardship, and toxic contamination. Historians of environmental justice note that pollution policy gradually expanded beyond questions Schlosberg and Carruthers, 2019 of industrial emissions to encompass unequal exposure, land rights, and community autonomy. The Standing Rock protests against the Dakota Access Pipeline, for example, helped shift national environmental discourse toward Indigenous governance and environmental racism.

This may, however, also reflect the persistence of environmental racism structures rather than the expansion of environmental inclusion. The growing presence of Indigenous terminology in environmental legislation suggests greater recognition of tribal sovereignty, but also emphasizes continued concentration of ecological risk Hoover et al., 2012 around Native lands. In this reading, the semantic emergence of “tribe” and “Indian” reflects the increasing visibility of longstanding inequalities linking pollution, extractive industry, and settler colonial governance. Environmental discourse thus expands beyond emissions and standards alone to address the uneven distribution of environmental harm across communities.

Discussion


This study demonstrates how computational linguistic methods can illuminate long-term ideological and institutional changes in American environmental governance. By applying topic modelling and word2vec models to a corpus of U.S. environmental legislation from 1973 onward, the project traced how the semantic associations surrounding the air particulates and term pollution evolved across five decades of environmental policymaking.

This also exhibits the value of natural language processing as a tool for historical analysis; word embeddings provide a method for detecting shifts in conceptual meaning that may not be immediately visible through close reading. For example, the changing semantic neighborhood of “pollution” illustrates how environmental legislation moved from a moral language of contamination and crisis toward a managerial language of governance and prevention. Computational methods therefore offer historians a way to analyze long-term conceptual change at vast scales.

There are, however, strong limitations to NLP. Legislative language does not necessarily reflect public opinion, activist discourse, or actual environmental outcomes. Because the corpus consists primarily of formal legal and regulatory texts, the semantic shifts observed here may reflect changes in bureaucratic style as much as changes in environmental conditions themselves. The applied methods also involve a degree of imprecision. Documents prior to 1983 were stored as hard-to-parse two-column files, resulting in word fragmentation in the text corpus. Thus, results from this time period may require further analysis of meaning. Finally, the strength of these methods is limited entirely to the text corpus in question. Legislation not annotated by congress.gov as directly environmental were not included in the training data for difficulty of data access. As a result, there are key gaps in areas of energy use, as well as in state- and local-level work. Future research could expand the dataset to include more of the “personal” perspective on the topic, such as environmental activist publications, to compare how different institutions conceptualized environmentalism over time.
